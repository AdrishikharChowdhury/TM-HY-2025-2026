\small\RaggedRight \textbf{\color{IEEEblue!75!black}Did you know?} \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.5pt,topsep=1pt,parsep=0pt,partopsep=0pt] \item Madhava of Sangamagrama derived infinite series for $\pi$ in 14th-century India, centuries before the same series reappeared in Europe as the Leibniz formula. \item Christoff Rudolff printed the radical sign $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ in 1525, possibly stylised from the letter $r$ for \textit{radix}, meaning root. \item John Wallis introduced the infinity symbol $\infty$ in 1655, while Leibniz gave calculus the elongated-S integral sign $\int$ in 1675. \item Thomas Harriot's \textit{Artis Analyticae Praxis}, published posthumously in 1631, gave the inequality signs $<$ and $>$ their first appearance in print. \item William Oughtred introduced the $\times$ multiplication sign in his \textit{Clavis Mathematicae} of 1631, the same year. \item Ren\'e Descartes fixed modern superscript exponent notation ($x^2$, $x^3$) in \textit{La G\'eom\'etrie} of 1637, alongside $x$, $y$, $z$ for unknowns. \item Brahmagupta's \textit{Brahmasphutasiddhanta} of 628 defined zero as the result of subtracting a number from itself and set rules for reckoning with negative numbers. \item Mu\d{h}ammad ibn M\=us\=a al-Khw\=arizm\={\i}'s \textit{Hisab al-jabr} (c.\ 825) gave algebra its name, from \textit{al-jabr}, completion. \item Leibniz introduced the $dx$ and $dy$ differential notation in a manuscript of November 1675. \item Euler gave the constant $e$ its letter in 1727--1731 and first used $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$ in 1777. \end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{center}
